A short guide to living roofs

Not planted. Composed.

Most people come to green roofs through one word: sedum. It is a good plant and a sensible place to start. Composed ecology goes further.

A composed roof is planned the way a good garden or a piece of music is planned: every part chosen for its place and its moment, and all of it working together. Everything on this page comes from roofs we have planted and watched grow, including our display roof at Larne Lough, planted in May 2026 and followed through its first summer.

Five principles

  1. Drawn from the landscape

    The planting palette comes from the ground the building stands on. Basalt grassland on the Antrim coast and machair on a Donegal shore suggest two different roofs.

  2. Planted in groups

    Plants that share the same depth, light and moisture are grouped together, the way they would grow in the wild. Each group feeds a different part of the roof's wildlife.

  3. Composed across the seasons

    The plan is set so something is in flower from early spring into autumn, with seed heads left standing through winter for birds and insects.

  4. Built with habitat

    Stone, sand and rubble are shaped into features that give insects places to nest, shelter, bask and drink.

  5. Labelled honestly

    Every plant is marked as a native or a garden guest, so you know exactly what is on your roof and why it is there.

    the ordinary British meadow, done right, on a roof.

Two ways to plant a roof

The layers underneath are the same. What arrives on the lorry is not.

Pre-grown blanket. Sedum blanket is grown in a nursery for a year or more on a mat, then cut, rolled and delivered like turf. The roof is fully covered on the day of installation. It is the most common green roof in Britain and Ireland, and we grow a great deal of it ourselves. It arrives finished, and its care goes into keeping it that way: a feed each spring and one or two maintenance visits a year are typical.

Plug planting. Plugs are young plants grown individually, each with its own root ball, and planted by hand into the substrate, around 25 to every square metre, following a plan drawn for the roof. Because each plant is placed one at a time, the roof can hold a far wider mix: wildflowers, herbs, grasses, coastal plants, and sedums too. It arrives young, and its care goes into the first year while it grows in. This is how every composed roof is planted.

One roof through its first summer

It arrives young, and it keeps getting better

A planted roof begins at its smallest. Everything after that is growth. It takes a full season to knit and a second to come into its own. From the third year it settles and seeds itself into its own gaps. Some plants move around. That is the roof working.

Annuals
Calendula and borage flower in the first summer, set seed, and come back the next year wherever they choose.
Biennials
Teasel and foxglove spend their first year as a leafy rosette and flower in their second summer. The teasel planted on our roof in May 2026 will flower in 2027.
The lasting carpet
Thyme, bird's-foot trefoil and fescue spread into a low permanent cover. Scabious and yarrow give it height and structure.
Winter
Seed heads of teasel, scabious and yarrow stand above straw-coloured grasses, and the stones hold the shape of the roof until the late-winter cut.

The display roof, group by group

These are the six groups on the roof in the photographs. Some follow a wild plant community closely. Others, such as the Kitchen Garden and the Night Garden, are built around a purpose. On a commissioned roof the groups are drawn from the vocabulary above and from your own landscape.

Wildflower Meadow
Scabious, yarrow, herb burnet, centaurea*, borage*, calendula*, bergamot*Open, nectar-rich flowers for bees and hoverflies through midsummer.
Pollinator Corridor
Creeping thyme, marjoram, kidney vetch, bird's-foot trefoil, hyssop*, aubrietia*A low flowering carpet. Bird's-foot trefoil is the main food plant of common blue caterpillars.
Kitchen Garden
Chives, oregano, alpine strawberry, sage*, garlic chives*, purslane*A roof you can pick from. Chive and oregano flowers are busy with bees.
Seasonal Drift
Teasel, bladder campion, aquilegia, scabious, dianthus*, blue fescue*Structure and movement. Teasel heads stand through winter, and goldfinches feed on the seed.
Night Garden
Bladder campion, sea campion, dianthus*, alyssum*Pale flowers that show up at dusk. The campions are visited by night-flying moths.
Woodland Edge
Foxglove, red campion, aquilegia, campanula*, winter purslane*For shaded roofs beside trees or taller buildings. Foxgloves are a favourite of bumblebees.

* Garden guest: a plant from outside Britain and Ireland, chosen for its flowers, its scent or its use in the kitchen. Sedum has a place here too: we grow Sedum album, reflexum and anglicum as plugs for the driest parts of a roof and the gaps between stones.

What the stones and sand are for

Plants feed wildlife. Habitat features give it somewhere to live. Each one is built from natural stone, sand or rubble and planted around its edges.

Stone Crevice
Slivers of stone set on edge with graded gaps between them. Sedums and thyme root into the cracks. The stone holds the sun's warmth, and insects shelter in the gaps.
Sand Bank
A patch of open sand with a planted margin. Many solitary bees nest in bare ground, and a sunny sand bank gives them a place to dig.
Rubble Reef
A low mound of broken stone, always set beside the sand bank. Beetles, spiders and overwintering insects find cover in the spaces between the pieces.
Dew Bowl
A shallow ring of stones that holds water for a while after rain. Damp-loving plants grow around it, and insects come to drink.

What a composed roof asks of you

If you look at the roof and think it might need water, it does. Water it.

That is the only watering rule we give. The system goes down in a day: drainage board, substrate, the plants set out by colour-coded box to the plan, a dressing of LECA, and a deep soak in. No specialist trade, no machinery. After that, most of the work comes early.

First summer
Water in dry weather while the roots establish. Pull out any weeds that blow in, while they are small. A walk across the roof every couple of weeks is enough.
Second summer
With hotter summers, the roof may want a little water in a long dry spell. Weeding becomes lighter as the plants close the gaps themselves.
Every year after
Leave the seed heads standing through winter. Then one tidy in late winter: cut back the old stems, take the clippings off the roof, and check the outlets and gravel borders are clear.

Section through a Natira roof, around 100mm to 125mm deep in total. Not to scale.

Weight. Our substrate weighs about 10kg per square metre for every 10mm of depth when fully soaked. At 100mm, the deepest we lay, that is around 100kg per square metre, plus about 20kg once the plants are established. The deepest parts of our display roof are 100mm, and thriving. Substrates vary, so ask any supplier for the saturated weight of theirs, and have your engineer confirm your roof can carry the load.

Five questions to ask any green roof supplier

  1. How deep is the substrate, and what does it weigh when saturated?

    Ask for a figure in kilograms per square metre, fully soaked. Your engineer needs it, and it tells you what can grow.

  2. What will the roof look like in year three?

    Ask to see photographs of a roof they supplied, taken a few years after installation as well as on the day it went on.

  3. Which plants are on it, and how many species?

    A named list tells you what will flower, when, and what it will feed.

  4. What care will it need, and who will do it?

    Ask how often it needs feeding, weeding or watering, and whether that is something you will do or a visit you will pay for.

  5. Where were the plants grown?

    Plants grown in a similar climate to yours settle faster and last longer.

Keep the outlets clear, give it one late-winter cut, and it will thicken rather than thin. The drifts will shift a little from year to year. That is the roof working. A composed ecology is meant to move.
